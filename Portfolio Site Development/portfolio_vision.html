ACA Medicaid Expansion · Staggered Difference-in-Differences

Staggered DiD analysis of the 2014 ACA Medicaid expansion using ACS (2010 to 2019) and BRFSS (2011 to 2019). Primary estimator is Callaway-Sant'Anna (2021) with never-expanding states as clean comparison; TWFE reported for benchmarking. Outcomes: uninsured rate and cost-related care avoidance.

Section 03

North Star

The Core Aspiration
"To be recognized as a researcher and analyst who translates rigorous empirical work into policy that meaningfully improves community outcomes, and to build a portfolio that demonstrates this capacity across methods, domains, and audiences."

The practical implication: every portfolio addition and skill developed should make you more capable of doing one or more of these things: designing a study, executing the analysis, interpreting findings honestly, and communicating them persuasively to decision-makers. The portfolio is not a record of past work alone; it is an argument about future capacity.

The doctoral track (if pursued) and the applied research / policy analyst track point toward overlapping skill sets. The distinguishing factors are: depth of methodological fluency, theoretical grounding in a substantive domain, and demonstrated ability to publish or produce high-stakes deliverables under constraint. Both tracks reward the same core competency: the ability to ask a good question, design an appropriate study, and communicate findings clearly.

Long-Term Vision · 6 to 24 Months

The long-term frame assumes one of two trajectories: (a) applied research and policy analyst roles in government, think tanks, or research nonprofits, or (b) doctoral study in public policy, urban planning, or a cognate field. These trajectories are not mutually exclusive: strong applied experience strengthens doctoral applications, and doctoral preparation strengthens applied research credibility.

6 to 12 Months

Establish a Substantive Niche

The portfolio should begin converging on 1 to 2 substantive domains (health insurance and health equity; disability and urban policy; mental health and public health) where you can speak with authority about the literature, the data landscape, and the policy environment. The Medicaid DiD project is a natural anchor for a health-coverage niche.

  • Read 20 to 30 key papers in your primary domain: build an annotated bibliography
  • Write one literature-informed policy analysis (not just a data project)
  • Develop a clear research question that could anchor a dissertation or thesis proposal
  • Attend at least one relevant conference (APPAM, APA, APHA, or similar)
9 to 15 Months

The gap between a strong policy analyst and a competitive doctoral candidate is often methodological depth, specifically causal inference. This is also what separates mid-level from senior research roles in applied settings.

  • Complete a rigorous course or self-study in causal inference (Mixtape, Angrist & Pischke) In progress via Medicaid DiD work
  • Add one difference-in-differences or instrumental variables analysis to portfolio Done

  • Begin learning spatial analysis (sf, tmap, spdep in R)

Section 06

Interview Readiness

The biggest gap in most early-career policy researcher interviews is the inability to narrate work in non-technical language without losing the substance. The goal is not to dumb things down, it is to identify the right level of abstraction for the audience and get there immediately.

Key Questions to Prepare

Question Type What They're Actually Asking What Your Answer Should Demonstrate
"Tell me about your research." Can you tell a coherent story about your work? Do you know why it matters? Lead with the policy problem, then the question, then the finding. Never lead with the data or method.
"What methods do you use?" Are you technically credible? Can you explain limitations? Name the methods (now including staggered DiD and Callaway-Sant'Anna), state what they answer and what they can't, show awareness of assumptions.
"How would you communicate findings to [non-technical audience]?" Are you a translator or only a producer? Give a concrete example. Reference your policy brief or stakeholder deck (once drafted).
"Why do you want to do research in [domain]?" Is this intrinsic or opportunistic? Will you stick with it? Connect to a specific experience, observation, or gap in the literature. Be specific.
"What's the most important limitation of your work?" Are you honest? Do you understand your own methods? Name a real limitation clearly (parallel trends assumption, small cohort sizes in the Medicaid DiD, etc.) and explain how it would change the conclusion.
"Walk me through how you'd approach [new policy problem]." Can you think analytically under uncertainty? Do you understand research design? Ask clarifying questions first. Then lay out: problem, question, data, design, interpretation, communication.
Core Interview Principle Reviewers do not remember your regression coefficients. They remember whether you knew why your work mattered and whether they would trust you to explain it to a city council member or a congressional staffer. Practice the non-technical version of every project until it is effortless.
Section 07

Guiding Principles

On Portfolio Development

A portfolio is an argument, not a record. Every piece should be selected and framed to support the claim: "I can identify important policy problems, bring rigorous evidence to bear on them, and communicate the implications to people who need to act on them." Remove or de-emphasize anything that doesn't support this claim. Strengthen everything that does.

On Skill Development

Learn methods in response to questions you can't yet answer, not in anticipation of questions that might arise. The most durable skill investments are those driven by a genuine analytical need: a dataset you want to analyze, a design problem you've identified in an existing project, or a paper you're trying to replicate. The Medicaid DiD is a textbook example: the staggered DiD literature was learned because the policy question required it.

On Pacing

Treat improvement as a practice, not a project. A policy brief written every three months, a new method learned every six, a conference attended annually: these compound invisibly but decisively. The researchers and analysts who stand out at the 5-year mark are usually the ones who kept steady progress over the first two years, not the ones who tried to do everything in a sprint.

On the Normative / Technical Balance

Your strongest comparative advantage is the combination of normative seriousness and empirical capability. Most technically skilled researchers struggle to articulate why their work matters. Most policy advocates struggle to evaluate evidence rigorously. Protect both sides of this balance: do not let technical skill development crowd out the reading, thinking, and writing in substantive domain knowledge that makes your analysis worth doing.
